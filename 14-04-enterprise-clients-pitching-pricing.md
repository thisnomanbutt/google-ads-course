## The Four Pricing Models

### Model 1: Percentage of spend

Typically 10–20% of ad spend, sometimes lower at high volume.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Scales with account size | **Incentive is misaligned** — you earn more by spending more |
| Simple to explain | Punishes you for improving efficiency |
| Industry standard | Clients increasingly resist it |

⚠️ **The misalignment is real and clients notice it.** If you recommend reducing spend (Lesson 8.1) your fee falls. Even if you always act honestly, the structure invites suspicion.

### Model 2: Flat monthly retainer

A fixed fee regardless of spend.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Aligned** — you can recommend reducing spend | Does not scale with account growth |
| Predictable for both sides | Scope creep risk |
| Simple | Needs renegotiation as the account grows |

**This is the best default for most relationships.**

### Model 3: Performance-based

A fee tied to results — cost per acquisition, revenue share, or profit share.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Fully aligned in theory | **Depends on factors you do not control** |
| Attractive to sceptical clients | Attribution disputes (Lesson 13.3) |
| Can be very lucrative | Client can damage results and you bear the cost |

⚠️ **Be extremely careful.** You do not control the offer, the pricing, the website, stock availability, the sales team, or the client's willingness to act on your advice.

**If you take performance pricing, need:**
- Agreement on the measurement method in writing (Lesson 13.3)
- A base retainer covering your costs
- Named client responsibilities, with consequences if unmet
- The right to exit if the client blocks agreed changes

### Model 4: Hybrid

A base retainer plus a performance component.

**Usually the best answer for larger engagements.** The base covers your cost and risk; the performance element aligns incentives without exposing you fully.

## Pricing the Work Properly

Most practitioners underprice, and the reason is that they price their time rather than the outcome.

### The wrong method

> "This takes about 10 hours a month, my rate is X, so the fee is 10X."

**The problem:** as you get better, the same work takes less time. **You would earn less for being more skilled.**

### The right method

> **Price against the value created, constrained by what the client can afford.**

### The calculation

From the audit (Lesson 13.4): you found PKR 340,000 a month of opportunity.

- If you capture even half of that: PKR 170,000 a month of extra profit
- A fee of PKR 60,000 leaves the client PKR 110,000 better off
- **That is a straightforward decision for them**

**Now compare to the time-based approach:** 12 hours at PKR 3,000 an hour = PKR 36,000.

**You would have charged 40% less for identical value.**

### The practical floor and ceiling

| | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Floor** | Never below your cost plus a sustainable margin |
| **Ceiling** | Never more than roughly a third of the value you can credibly create |
| **Sense check** | The client must clearly profit from working with you |

⚠️ **If you cannot find enough value to justify a workable fee, the honest answer is that this client is too small for you.** Say so rather than taking work you cannot serve properly.

## Pitching Without Competing on Price

From Lesson 13.4, the audit does most of the work. Here is the rest.

### The structure

**1. Lead with findings, not credentials.**
Your audit, their account, their money. Sixty seconds in.

**2. Set up the gap.**
"You are currently achieving X. Here is what is achievable, and why."

**3. Show the mechanism.**
Not "we will optimise your account" but the specific changes and why each works.

**4. Be honest about what you cannot fix.**
"Your refund rate is costing more than anything I can do in the ad account. That is an operations problem, and I will flag it but I cannot solve it."

⚠️ **This single move separates you from every competitor.** Everyone else claims they can fix everything.

**5. Price it against the value, stated plainly.**

**6. Make the decision easy.**
A short trial period, a clear scope, and a straightforward exit.

### When you are the expensive option

You will be. Handle it directly.

> "I am more expensive. Here is what that buys: [specific capability the others do not have]. If cost per lead matters more than measurement quality, the cheaper option may really be right for you."

**Being willing to lose the work on price is what makes the price credible.**

### What not to do

❌ Discount to win. You will be discounted forever, and they will value you at the discounted price.
❌ Match a competitor's scope you think is wrong.
❌ Promise a specific result. You do not control enough variables (Lesson 0.7).
❌ Criticise the incumbent (Lesson 13.4).

## What Enterprise Clients Need Differently

Larger clients are not just bigger. The relationship is structurally different.

### 1. More stakeholders

A small business has one decision maker. An enterprise has a marketing manager, a marketing director, finance, procurement, legal and often an internal analytics team.

**Each has different questions.**

| Stakeholder | Cares about |
|---|---|
| Marketing manager | Day-to-day performance, whether their targets are met |
| Marketing director | Channel strategy, budget allocation, board reporting |
| Finance | Contribution margin, spend control, invoicing accuracy |
| Analytics | Measurement method, data quality |
| Procurement | Contract terms, cost benchmarking |
| Legal | Data processing, privacy compliance |

**Build a version of your reporting for each** (Lesson 8.2). One report serving all six serves none.

### 2. Slower decisions

A change that takes a small business ten minutes takes an enterprise six weeks and three approvals.

**Plan for this.** Build the approval timeline into your project plans, and never assume a decision is made because someone in a meeting agreed.

### 3. Process and documentation what you need

Change control, security reviews, data processing agreements, insurance, supplier onboarding portals.

**This is real work and it should be priced in.**

### 4. Higher measurement scrutiny

An enterprise analytics team will challenge your numbers. **This is good.** It is also why Modules 12 and 13 matter — you need to be able to defend your method, not just your results.

### 5. Political awareness

Someone internally chose the incumbent. Someone has a preferred channel. Someone's bonus depends on a metric you are about to prove is wrong.

⚠️ **The finding from Lesson 13.2 — that brand search is overvalued — is technically simple and politically difficult.** Somebody built that campaign and reports its ROAS upward.

**Handle it by sharing credit and framing forward:** "The data suggests we can free budget here for higher-return work" lands better than "this campaign has been wasting money".

## The Turnaround Process

You inherit an account in trouble, or an existing account deteriorates. Here is the sequence.

### Week 1: Stabilise and diagnose

**Do not optimise yet.**

1. **Stop the bleeding.** Pause anything clearly and severely unprofitable. Nothing else.
2. **Run the full audit** (Lesson 13.4).
3. **Check tracking before believing any data** (Lesson 2.5).
4. **Set up the true baseline** using business numbers, not platform numbers.
5. **Set expectations in writing:** "The first 30 days are diagnosis and foundation. Performance may not improve, and may temporarily worsen."

⚠️ **That last point is essential.** A turnaround that begins with a month of no improvement will fail politically unless it was predicted.

### Weeks 2–4: Fix the foundations

**In this order, always:**

1. Conversion tracking (Module 2, Module 7)
2. What is being counted as a conversion (Lesson 2.3)
3. Obvious waste — locations, placements, loss-making products
4. Policy and compliance risk (Lesson 1.5)

**Not bids. Not keywords. Not ad copy.** Those come later and are meaningless without correct measurement.

### Weeks 5–8: Structure and stabilise

5. Campaign structure if really broken (Lesson 1.4)
6. Bid strategy alignment with data volume (Lesson 5.1)
7. **Then stop changing things for 21 days** (Lesson 5.2)

⚠️ **The hardest part of a turnaround is the enforced stability period**, because everyone wants visible activity.

### Weeks 9–16: Optimise

8. Quality Score programme (Lesson 4.5)
9. Landing pages (Lesson 0.5)
10. Scaling decisions from impression share (Lesson 8.1)
11. Testing programme (Lesson 13.1)

### The communication rhythm

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Day 1 | The plan and the honest timeline |
| Day 7 | Audit findings, prioritised by money |
| Weekly, weeks 1–8 | Short written update: what was done, what is next |
| Day 30 | Foundation review — what is fixed, what remains |
| Day 90 | First honest performance check |

**Over-communicate during a turnaround.** Silence during a period of no visible improvement is how turnarounds get cancelled at week six.

## When to Resign a Client

Sometimes the right decision is to leave.

### The genuine reasons

**1. They will not fix what must be fixed.**
You have shown that tracking is broken or the offer is uncompetitive, repeatedly, and they will not act. You cannot succeed and you will be blamed.

**2. They demand something unethical.**
Misleading claims, ignoring consent what you need, or a technique that risks their account (Lesson 1.5). Explain the risk once, in writing. If they insist, leave.

**3. Chronic interference.**
The client makes changes you did not agree, restarting learning phases constantly (Lesson 5.2). You cannot deliver and you will be held responsible.

**4. The economics do not work — for either party.**
The fee does not cover the work, or the value you can create does not justify the fee.

**5. The relationship is abusive.**
Life is short.

### How to resign well

1. **Give proper notice**, per your contract (Lesson 0.7)
2. **Deliver the full handover pack** (Lesson 10.1)
3. **Do not sabotage anything.** Ever.
4. **Be honest but not cruel** about why
5. **Offer a recommendation** to someone better suited, if you can

⚠️ **Your industry is smaller than you think.** How you leave is remembered longer than how you performed.

### The reason not to resign

❌ **"The results are bad."**

If the results are bad and the cause is fixable, that is the job. Leaving because performance is difficult is what makes a supplier; staying and fixing it is what makes an advisor.

## 📖 Real-World Example: The Turnaround and the Resignation

Bilal took on two troubled accounts in the same quarter. One succeeded. He resigned the other.

### Account 1: The turnaround

A UK B2B services company, £34,000 monthly spend, four agencies in three years. Cost per lead had risen from £180 to £420.

**The client's brief:** "Fix it in 30 days."

**His response, in writing, before signing:**

> "I cannot fix it in 30 days, and anyone who says they can is guessing. Here is what will actually happen. Days 1–30 are diagnosis and foundations. Performance will not improve and may briefly worsen. Days 30–90 are where improvement appears. If that timeline does not work, we should not work together."

**They almost did not hire him.** They did, because nobody else had been honest about the timeline.

**What the audit found:**
- Conversion tracking counting form views, not submissions — **every number in three years of reporting was wrong**
- Six Primary conversion actions
- Location targeting including all of Europe
- 42% of spend on informational keywords
- No negative keyword list at all
- Four agencies had each restructured the account, leaving fragments of all four structures

**Week 1–4:** tracking rebuilt and checked. Conversion actions reduced to one. Locations fixed. Informational keywords paused.

**Reported conversions fell 71%** — because the previous number had been counting form views.

**This was the dangerous moment.** The marketing manager panicked in week 3.

Because Bilal had predicted it in writing, and because he sent a short update every Friday, they held.

**Week 5–8:** structure rebuilt into four campaigns. Bid strategy set. **Then 21 days of nothing.**

**Week 9–16:** Quality Score work, two new landing pages, scaling.

**Results at day 120:**

| Metric | Day 0 | Day 120 |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly spend | £34,000 | £34,000 |
| Reported conversions | 81 | 62 |
| **Actual qualified leads** | 34 | **119** |
| **Cost per qualified lead** | **£1,000** | **£286** |

**Reported conversions fell 23%. Real qualified leads more than tripled.**

**The client's comment:** *"You were the only one who told us it would get worse first. That is the only reason we believed you when it got better."*

### Account 2: The resignation

A Pakistani e-commerce business, PKR 800,000 monthly spend. Strong products, growing fast.

**The problem, found in week 2:** their conversion tracking double-counted every order (Lesson 2.4 — no transaction ID, a hardcoded tag plus a GTM tag). Their reported ROAS of 6.2x was actually 3.1x.

Below their breakeven of 3.4x.

**He showed them the workings. They agreed it was correct.**

**Then they asked him not to fix it.**

Their reasoning: they were raising investment. The 6.2x ROAS was in the investor deck. Fixing it before the round closed would be inconvenient.

### What he did

**First, he wrote it down.** A clear email: the tracking is double-counting, the real ROAS is 3.1x, this is below breakeven, and the campaigns are losing money on every sale. He recommended fixing it immediately.

**They declined in writing.**

**He resigned the following week.**

His resignation note was short and factual:

> "I cannot manage an account where I know the measurement is wrong and have been asked not to correct it. Every decision I make would be based on numbers I know to be false. I have documented the issue and the fix in the handover pack. I am happy to recommend someone else, and I wish you well with the raise."

### What happened afterwards

Fourteen months later, the founder contacted him. The investment had closed. Six months after that, the investors' own analysts found the double counting.

The business had scaled spend aggressively on the false figures and had accumulated significant losses. The relationship with the investors was badly damaged.

**The founder's message:** *"You were right, and I should have listened. Would you consider working with us again?"*

**He said yes** — with one condition written into the contract: measurement integrity is not negotiable, and he has the unilateral right to correct any tracking error he finds.

### The two lessons

**From the turnaround:** honesty about a difficult timeline is what earns permission to do the work properly. Everyone else promised 30 days and would have failed in 30 days.

**From the resignation:** he lost PKR 96,000 a month in fees by leaving. He got the client back at a higher fee, with better terms, and with a reputation that brought other work.

⚠️ **He did not resign to win the client back later.** That was not the plan and it was not likely. He resigned because he could not do the job honestly.

**The commercial benefit was a consequence, not a motive.** But it is worth knowing that integrity is not usually as expensive as it feels in the moment.

## 📌 Key Takeaways

- Flat retainers align incentives best for most relationships. Percentage of spend rewards you for spending more.
- Price against value created, not hours worked. Improving efficiency should not reduce your income.
- Being honest about what you cannot fix separates you from every competitor who claims they can fix everything.
- In a turnaround, fix tracking first, then what is counted, then waste. Never bids first.
- Predict the difficult period in writing before it happens. That is what earns the patience to complete the work.

## 🎯 Expert Challenge

Build your commercial operating model.

- Design your pricing structure with the model, the floor, the ceiling and the review trigger as an account grows. Include how you will handle scope creep.
- Build the pitch process around the audit: the deliverable, the recording, the follow-up, and the specific point at which you discuss fees.
- Design the enterprise engagement model: stakeholder map, reporting per audience, approval timelines, and the documentation what you need priced in.
- Build the turnaround process as a formal document with week-by-week actions, communication schedule, and the written expectation-setting language you will use on day one.
- Write your engagement principles: the things you will not do, the conditions you need, and the circumstances under which you would resign. Put them in your contract.
- Then test them. The next time a client asks for something that crosses one of those lines, hold it. **Principles written down and never tested are not principles.** The first time you hold one will cost you something, and it will change how you are treated for the rest of your career.
