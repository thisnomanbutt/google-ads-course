## The Three Forces

Almost every change in paid media comes from one of three forces.

### Force 1: Privacy

Regulation and browser restrictions keep reducing what can be tracked about individuals.

**Direction of travel:** less individual-level data, more aggregate and modelled data.

**What this has already produced:** Enhanced Conversions (Lesson 7.1), Consent Mode (Lesson 7.5), server-side tagging (Lesson 7.3), the rise of first-party data (Lesson 12.1), and the return of MMM (Lesson 12.4).

**What it will keep producing:** more modelling, more aggregation, more emphasis on data you own.

### Force 2: Automation

Google keeps moving decisions from the advertiser to the machine.

**The progression is clear:** manual bids → automated bids → automated targeting → automated creative → automated campaign types.

**What this has already produced:** Smart Bidding, broad match plus AI, Performance Max, automatically created assets, AI-generated creative.

**What it will keep producing:** fewer levers, more emphasis on inputs and objectives.

### Force 3: Interface change

How people search keeps changing.

**What this has already produced:** mobile-first search, voice search, AI Overviews (Lesson 1.1), conversational queries.

**What it will keep producing:** more answers given without a click, more conversational interaction, new surfaces.

**Every specific change you will face for the rest of your career is one of these three forces expressing itself in a new product.**

## Privacy: The Structural View

Rather than tracking individual announcements, understand the structure.

### What is disappearing

- Third-party cookies (with a longer and more complicated history than anyone predicted)
- Cross-site individual tracking
- Device identifiers
- Detailed demographic targeting in sensitive categories
- Detailed, user-level reporting

### What is replacing it

| Old approach | Replacement |
|---|---|
| Third-party cookies | First-party data (Lesson 12.1) |
| Individual attribution | Aggregate modelling and experiments (Lessons 12.4, 13.2) |
| Pixel-based tracking | Server-side and API-based (Lessons 7.3, 7.4) |
| Observed conversions | Observed plus modelled conversions (Lesson 7.5) |
| Cross-site audiences | Contextual and first-party audiences |

### What this means for you

**The advertisers who suffer are those who depended on tracking individuals across the web.**

**The advertisers who benefit are those with:**
- Real customer relationships and a customer list
- Direct measurement of business outcomes
- Willingness to run experiments
- Good offers that do not need surveillance to sell

⚠️ **Privacy change is not a threat to good advertising. It is a threat to lazy advertising.**

### On Privacy Sandbox specifically

Google has spent years developing privacy-preserving alternatives to third-party cookies, under the Privacy Sandbox programme. The plans, timelines and even the fundamental approach have changed repeatedly.

**Do not build a strategy around any specific Privacy Sandbox technology.** Build around first-party data, which works regardless of which technical approach prevails.

## Automation: What It Means for Your Job

The honest question people ask: **will automation make this job disappear?**

### What automation has taken

| Task | Status |
|---|---|
| Setting individual keyword bids | Gone |
| Manual device and schedule adjustments | Largely gone |
| Writing one ad and testing it against another | Largely gone |
| Manual placement selection | Mostly gone |
| Manual audience combination | Reducing |

**These were skills. They are now obsolete.** Anyone whose value was in doing them by hand has already been replaced.

### What automation cannot take

| Capability | Why |
|---|---|
| Knowing what a customer is worth | The machine does not have your P&L (Lesson 0.3) |
| Judging whether an offer is good | Needs market and human understanding (Lesson 0.2) |
| Deciding what to measure | The machine optimises what you choose (Lesson 2.3) |
| Diagnosing why something broke | Needs reasoning across systems (Lesson 8.4) |
| Knowing when the data is lying | Needs scepticism (Lessons 7.1, 9.5) |
| Deciding whether to enter a market | Business judgement (Lesson 14.1) |
| Explaining it to a business owner | Communication (Lesson 8.2) |
| Designing an experiment | Needs understanding causation (Lesson 13.1) |
| Recommending against your own budget | Needs integrity (Lesson 13.3) |

**Every item in that second list is in this course, and none of them is a Google Ads feature.**

### The shape of the change

> **Automation removes the execution and increases the value of the judgement.**

**Think of the change in accountancy when spreadsheets arrived.** Bookkeepers who added up columns by hand were replaced. Accountants who advised on structure, tax and strategy became more valuable, because the tedious part stopped consuming their time.

**Paid media is in the same transition.** Media buyers who set bids are being replaced. Marketers who understand economics, measurement and business are becoming more valuable.

**This is why Module 0 came first.**

## How to Evaluate Any New Feature

You will face a new product or feature every few months, each announced enthusiastically. Here is a framework that does not go out of date.

### The seven questions

**1. What problem does it solve?**
If you cannot state the problem in one sentence, be cautious.

**2. Do I have that problem?**
Many features solve problems you do not have.

**3. What does it replace?**
If it replaces something working well, the bar is higher.

**4. What control do I give up?**
Automation almost always trades control for convenience. Is that trade good here?

**5. How would I measure whether it worked?**
If you cannot design the test (Lesson 13.1), you cannot judge the feature.

**6. What is the downside case?**
If it fails, how bad is it and how fast can you reverse it?

**7. Who benefits?**
Google's interests and your client's interests overlap a lot but not completely (Lesson 10.4).

### The adoption rule

> **Never adopt a new feature across a whole account. Test it in a contained campaign with a defined measurement window and a rollback plan.**

**This rule has never been wrong**, for any feature, in any year.

## The Five Things That Will Still Matter

Whatever changes, these will not.

### 1. Unit economics

Someone must know what a customer is worth and what you can pay to get one.

**No automation removes this**, because the machine does not have access to your costs unless you give them to it (Lesson 12.3).

### 2. Offer quality

Advertising amplifies. It does not fix.

**A better offer will outperform a better account, every time** (Lesson 0.2). This has been true since advertising existed.

### 3. Measurement integrity

The machine optimises what you measure. If you measure the wrong thing, it will pursue the wrong thing with great efficiency (Lesson 11.1).

**Deciding what to measure will always be a human judgement.**

### 4. Causal thinking

Attribution will keep degrading. Experiments will keep working.

**The ability to design a test that proves causation** (Lessons 13.1, 13.2) becomes more valuable as correlation-based measurement becomes less reliable.

### 5. Communication

Someone must explain to a business owner what is happening and what to do.

**This has never been automatable and it is the skill most practitioners neglect** (Lesson 8.2).

## What to Do About the Future, Practically

### Build the things that survive

| Investment | Why it survives |
|---|---|
| First-party data (Lesson 12.1) | Independent of any platform |
| Offline conversion loops (Lesson 7.4) | Works regardless of tracking changes |
| A data warehouse (Lesson 7.6) | You own the data permanently |
| Experimentation capability (Lesson 13.1) | Method, not tool |
| Business economics knowledge (Module 0) | Never changes |
| Client relationships (Lesson 8.2) | Not a technology |

### Be cautious about

| Investment | Why |
|---|---|
| Deep expertise in one campaign type | Campaign types change |
| Custom tooling that duplicates free tools | Maintenance burden (Lesson 11.3) |
| Tactics dependent on a specific loophole | Loopholes close |
| Anything needing individual cross-site tracking | Direction of travel is against it |

### The portfolio approach

**Spend roughly 70% of your learning time on principles and 30% on current features.**

Principles compound over a career. Features expire.

## 📖 Real-World Example: Two Careers, Ten Years

Two people started in paid search in the same year, at the same agency.

### Person A: the platform expert

They became really excellent at Google Ads mechanics. They knew every setting, every bid adjustment, every keyword-level lever. They could build a 400-ad-group SKAG account faster than anyone.

**They were, for several years, the best media buyer in the agency.**

Their learning went into new features. When a new campaign type launched, they knew it first. When a new bid adjustment appeared, they had tested it within a week.

**What happened:**

- **Year 3:** Smart Bidding removed most manual bid work. Their main skill lost value.
- **Year 5:** Responsive Search Ads removed manual ad testing. SKAGs became counterproductive.
- **Year 6:** broad match plus Smart Bidding began outperforming their tightly controlled structures.
- **Year 8:** PMax removed most of the remaining levers.

Each time, they learned the new feature quickly. **But each new feature needed less skill than the one it replaced.**

By year 10 they were competent, current, and struggling to explain why they were worth more than someone with two years' experience. They were still doing execution, and execution had become easy.

### Person B: the business thinker

They were slower at the platform. They asked what they considered obvious questions, and got mildly irritating answers about "just set the bid".

Their learning went elsewhere:

- **Year 2:** learned to read a P&L, so they understood client margins
- **Year 3:** learned enough SQL to query data directly
- **Year 4:** ran their first geo experiment, badly, and learned from it
- **Year 5:** built their first offline conversion pipeline
- **Year 6:** learned to write clearly, because their reports were being ignored
- **Year 7:** built their first MMM
- **Year 8:** started recommending things outside advertising, including one client's refund process
- **Year 10:** running measurement strategy across all channels for three large businesses

**Each platform change affected them less**, because their value was not in the levers.

### Where they are now

**Person A** manages a portfolio of accounts at a good agency. Competent, respected, and priced against every other competent practitioner in the market.

**Person B** is a consultant. Fewer clients, much higher fees, and a waiting list. Their clients call them about pricing decisions, market entry and measurement — questions that have nothing to do with Google Ads.

### The uncomfortable observation

**Person A was better at Google Ads for the first five years.** Measurably better. If you had asked either of them in year three who was more skilled, both would have said Person A.

**The skill was real. It just depreciated.**

### What Person B did differently

Not talent. A different allocation of learning time.

Person A spent perhaps 90% of their learning on current features. Person B spent perhaps 30%.

**Over ten years, that ratio compounds into two entirely different careers.**

⚠️ **Note carefully:** Person B still had to be competent at the platform. Ignorance of the mechanics is not a strategy. **They were good enough at execution to be credible, and excellent at judgement.**

That is the combination this course has been building.

## 📌 Key Takeaways

- Every change comes from one of three forces: privacy, automation, or how people search.
- Privacy change threatens lazy advertising, not good advertising. First-party data is the answer regardless of which specific technology prevails.
- Automation removes execution and increases the value of judgement. This is why Module 0 came first.
- Evaluate every new feature with the seven questions, and never adopt one account-wide without a contained test.
- Five things will still matter: unit economics, offer quality, measurement integrity, causal thinking and communication.

## 💪 Practical Exercise (30 minutes)

2. Calculate the ratio. If features exceed 60%, that is your finding.
3. Score yourself 1–10 on each of the five durable skills.
4. Find your weakest, and write one concrete action to improve it this quarter.
5. List any capability you have that depends on a specific current feature. What happens if it changes?

## 🎯 Expert Challenge

Build a personal and organisational resilience plan.

- Map every capability you or your team relies on, and classify each as durable or feature-dependent. Feature-dependent capabilities are liabilities disguised as strengths.
- For each feature-dependent capability, find what would replace it if the feature disappeared, and how long the transition would take.
- Build the learning allocation plan with a deliberate ratio between principles and features, and hold yourself to it for a quarter.
- Design the feature evaluation process as a written standard, so that new products are assessed rather than adopted enthusiastically.
- Build the durable asset inventory for each client: first-party data, offline conversion loops, warehouse, experimentation history, documented economics. Score each client and find the gaps.
- Then do the honest exercise. Imagine Google removes every remaining manual lever tomorrow — one input field for your objective, one for your budget, one for your creative. **Write down what you would still be paid for.** If the answer is short, that is the most useful thing you will learn this year, and there is time to act on it.
