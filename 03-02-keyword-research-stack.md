## Before You Open Any Tool

Most people open Keyword Planner first. That is a mistake. You will end up with a list of what *Google thinks* is related, not what your *customers* actually want.

**Start with these three questions, on paper:**

1. **What problem does this business solve?** Write it in the customer's words, not the company's words.
2. **What does the customer call it?** Businesses say "water purification system". Customers say "water filter".
3. **What happens right before someone buys?** What triggers the search? A leaking pipe. A toothache. A failed project deadline.

**Think of a doctor.** A good doctor asks questions before reaching for the test equipment. The tests confirm or correct the hypothesis — they do not replace thinking.

Write 20 keywords from your head first. Then open the tools.

## Tool 1: Google Keyword Planner (Free)

The official tool. Free with any Google Ads account.

### What it is really good for

- **Discovering keywords you had not thought of**
- **Seeing which terms Google groups together** — this tells you how the AI thinks
- **Rough cost estimates** for budgeting conversations
- **Seasonality data** — the monthly trend graph is really useful

### Where it misleads you

⚠️ **1. Volume ranges, not real numbers.** Without spend history, you see bands like "1K–10K". That range is almost useless for planning.

⚠️ **2. Grouped volumes.** Keyword Planner often merges close variants into one number. "Water filter" and "water filters" may show the same volume because they are counted together.

⚠️ **3. The suggestions are commercially motivated.** It shows keywords Google can sell you, prioritising what it can monetise.

⚠️ **4. The cost estimates are wide.** The "top of page bid" range is a rough guide, nothing more. Your actual cost depends on your Quality Score (Lesson 1.1).

### How to use it properly

1. Go to **Tools → Planning → Keyword Planner → Discover new keywords**
2. Enter **your website URL**, not just seed keywords. Google will extract themes from your actual site.
3. Then run it again with your **competitor's URL**. This is one of the most useful free features in Google Ads.
4. Set the location and language correctly. Default settings will give you US data for a Karachi business.
5. Filter out keywords with very low relevance rather than downloading 4,000 rows.
6. Download to CSV and grade by intent (Lesson 3.1) before doing anything else.

## Tool 2: The Free Sources Most People Ignore

These cost nothing and are often better than paid tools.

### Google Search Console
Real queries where your site already appears. Not estimates — **actual data**.

*How to use it:* find queries with impressions but low clicks, and where your organic position is 5 or worse. These are proven-demand keywords where ads can win immediately.

### Google Autocomplete
Type your keyword and watch the suggestions. These are real, common searches.

*Pro move:* type your keyword followed by each letter of the alphabet. "water filter a", "water filter b"... You will find phrases no tool suggests.

### "People also ask" and "Related searches"
At the bottom and middle of any results page. Free intent data, straight from Google.

### Your own search terms report
The single best source, once you are running. What real people actually typed to trigger your ads. This is Lesson 3.4.

### Your customers
Ask sales: *"What words do customers use when they call?"* Ask support: *"What do people ask before buying?"* Read your own reviews.

This is where you find the phrases nobody else is bidding on.

### Competitor websites
Read their homepage, their product pages, their FAQ. The words they chose are usually words their customers used.

### Reddit, Facebook groups, forums
Read how real people describe the problem in their own words. This is where you find Level 3 comparison language.

## Tool 3: Paid Tools (SEMrush, Ahrefs and similar)

### What they really add

1. **Competitor paid keyword lists.** See which keywords a competitor is bidding on, and roughly what they spend.
2. **Competitor ad copy history.** See the ads they have been running, and for how long. An ad running for two years is almost certainly profitable — that is free market research.
3. **Better volume data** — actual numbers rather than ranges, and less grouping.
4. **Keyword difficulty and SERP analysis** — useful for coordinating paid and organic.
5. **Historical trends** over years, not months.

### The honest question: do you need one?

| Situation | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Learning, one small account | **No.** Free tools are enough. Save the money. |
| Freelancer, 2–3 clients | Maybe. Consider a monthly subscription only in months you do research. |
| Agency with 5+ clients | **Yes.** The competitor data saves real hours. |
| Large single account, mature | Yes, mainly for competitor monitoring |

⚠️ **A caution:** these tools estimate. Their "competitor spend" figures are models, not facts. Treat them as directional, not precise. Never quote a competitor's estimated spend to a client as a fact.

## Competitor Mining: The Practical Process

This is legal, normal and expected. Everyone does it. Here is how to do it well.

### Step 1: Find the real competitors

Not who the client *thinks* competes with them. Use **Auction Insights** in Google Ads (Campaigns → Insights → Auction insights). This shows who actually appears alongside your ads.

You will often find a competitor the client had never heard of.

### Step 2: Manual SERP review (free, 20 minutes)

1. Search your top 5 keywords in an incognito window, with location set correctly
2. Screenshot every ad
3. Open every landing page
4. Record: headline, offer, price, guarantee, call to action

This is the Lesson 0.2 teardown, applied to keywords.

### Step 3: Keyword Planner on their domain (free)

Enter their URL in "Discover new keywords". Google will show themes from their site.

### Step 4: Paid tool extraction (if you have one)

Pull their paid keyword list. Sort by estimated traffic. Grade by intent.

### Step 5: The gap analysis

Build a simple table:

| Keyword | You bid? | Competitor A | Competitor B | Intent | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| water filter price karachi | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | 4 | Defend |
| water filter without electricity | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ | 4 | **Opportunity** |
| ro plant for home | ❌ | ✅ | ✅ | 4 | **Test** |
| how to clean water filter | ✅ | ❌ | ❌ | 1 | **Remove** |

That last row matters as much as the others. If **no** competitor bids on a keyword you are spending on, ask why. Sometimes you found a gap. More often, they tested it and it failed.

## Judging a Keyword: The Four Questions

Before adding any keyword, ask:

**1. What is the intent level?** (Lesson 3.1) Below Level 3, justify it.

**2. Can I afford it?** Compare the estimated cost per click to your maximum cost per sale (Lesson 0.3). If the top-of-page bid is PKR 200 and your maximum cost per sale is PKR 2,600, you need a conversion rate above 7.7% to break even. Is that realistic? Usually not.

**3. Do I have a page that matches it?** If someone searches "water filter without electricity" and lands on a general page, you will lose. Message match, from Lesson 0.5.

**4. Is there enough volume to matter?** A perfect keyword with 20 searches a month is a nice detail, not a strategy. Do not spend two hours building a campaign around it.

## The 90-Minute Research Process

Here is the complete workflow. Follow it in order.

**Minutes 0–15: Thinking.** Answer the three questions on paper. Write 20 keywords from your head.

**Minutes 15–30: Google Keyword Planner.** Your URL, then competitor URLs. Export everything.

**Minutes 30–45: Free sources.** Autocomplete, People Also Ask, Search Console. Add to the list.

**Minutes 45–60: Competitor SERP review.** Screenshot ads, open landing pages, record offers.

**Minutes 60–75: Grade and filter.** Add intent level 1–5 to every keyword. Remove everything you cannot afford or cannot serve with a matching page.

**Minutes 75–90: Cluster.** Group the survivors into intent themes. This becomes your ad group structure (Lesson 3.5).

**Output:** a filtered, graded, clustered keyword list — not a 4,000-row spreadsheet nobody will ever read.

## 📖 Real-World Example: The Keyword Nobody Was Bidding On

Sana ran the 90-minute process for her water filter store. Here is what happened at each stage.

**Thinking stage.** She wrote down what customers actually said when they called. One phrase kept appearing: *"Does it work when there is load-shedding?"*

Electricity supply is unreliable in parts of Karachi. Her filter was gravity-based — **it needed no electricity at all.** She had never advertised this. It was mentioned once, in the specifications table, near the bottom of the page.

**Keyword Planner stage.** She found the obvious terms: "water filter karachi" (high volume, high competition), "water purifier price" (high volume, high competition).

**Autocomplete stage.** She typed "water filter without" and Google suggested:
- water filter without electricity
- water filter without installation
- water filter without plumbing

**Competitor review stage.** She checked the top 6 competitors' ads. **Not one** mentioned electricity. Every single ad said the same things: "Best quality", "Free delivery", "Affordable price".

This was the Lesson 0.2 gap, found in keyword data.

**What she built:**

- New ad group: `Problem - No Electricity`
- Keywords: water filter without electricity, non electric water filter, gravity water filter, water filter for load shedding, manual water purifier
- New landing page with the headline: **"Clean Water Even During Load-Shedding. No Electricity Needed."**
- Ad headline matching exactly (message match, Lesson 0.5)

**Results after 8 weeks:**

| Metric | This ad group | Account average |
|---|---|---|
| Cost per click | PKR 22 | PKR 40 |
| Conversion rate | 6.1% | 2.4% |
| Cost per sale | **PKR 361** | PKR 1,667 |
| Monthly volume | 34 sales | — |

**Cost per sale was 4.6 times better than her account average.**

Why so cheap? Low competition, because nobody else had noticed. And high conversion, because the ad answered the exact fear the searcher had.

**The volume was modest** — 34 sales a month, not 300. But it was the most profitable ad group in the account, and it came from one sentence a customer said on the phone.

**The lesson:** the best keyword she ever found did not come from a tool. It came from listening. The tools only confirmed it and gave her the variants.

## 📌 Key Takeaways

- Think before you open a tool. Write 20 keywords from your head first.
- Keyword Planner shows ranges, groups variants, and is commercially motivated. Use it, but do not trust the numbers exactly.
- The best free sources are Search Console, autocomplete, your own search terms and your own customers.
- Use Auction Insights to find who really competes with you, then tear down their ads and pages.
- Filter every keyword against four questions: intent, affordability, matching page, and meaningful volume.

3. Use autocomplete with five different starting phrases.

## 🎯 Expert Challenge

Build a complete competitive keyword intelligence report.

- Find the true competitor set using Auction Insights, and segment them: direct competitors, marketplace aggregators, and comparison sites. Each needs a different response.
- Build the full gap matrix across at least 100 keywords, marked with who bids on what.
- For each gap, estimate the opportunity: volume × realistic conversion rate × your margin. Rank them by value, not by curiosity.
- Analyse competitor ad copy for patterns. What claim does everyone make? What does nobody say? (Lesson 0.2 applied at scale.)
- Then produce a one-page strategy: three keywords to defend, three gaps to attack, and three keywords to abandon — each with the reasoning and the expected profit impact.
- Finally, set up a monitoring routine so you notice within a month when a competitor enters a keyword you own.
