## The Roles, in Order of Need

Do not hire a full team. Hire in this sequence, and only when the limit is real.

### Hire 1: The Operator

**What they do:** execute the daily and weekly work. Search terms, negatives, asset reviews, budget checks, reporting.

**What they need:** Modules 1–5. Reliability matters more than brilliance.

**When to hire:** when execution work exceeds roughly 25 hours a week and is limiting your strategic time.

⚠️ **Do not hire a strategist first.** A strategist with no operator becomes an expensive operator.

### Hire 2: The Analyst

**What they do:** measurement, reporting, data pipelines, experiment design.

**What they need:** Modules 7, 8, 12, 13. Comfortable with spreadsheets and ideally SQL.

**When to hire:** when measurement work is being skipped because nobody has time, or when you have more than about 8 accounts.

**This is often the highest-value second hire**, and it is usually made too late.

### Hire 3: The Creative

**What they do:** ad copy, landing page briefs, video, image assets.

**What they need:** Modules 0 and 4. Writing ability matters more than platform knowledge.

**When to hire:** when creative is the limit — usually once you move into Demand Gen and video (Lessons 6.4, 6.5).

### Hire 4: The Second Operator

Only now do you duplicate the first role.

### Hire 5: The Strategist / Account Lead

**What they do:** client relationships, strategy, the difficult conversations, audits.

**What they need:** everything, especially Modules 0, 12 and 13.

**When to hire:** when you personally are the limit on client relationships.

⚠️ **This role is usually you, until it cannot be.** Hiring it too early creates a layer between you and your clients that damages both.

## Hiring When You Cannot Assess Skill

A common problem: a business owner needs a media buyer but cannot judge whether a candidate is any good.

### What does not work

❌ **Certifications.** Free, and largely a test of whether someone can pass a multiple-choice exam. Necessary, not enough.

❌ **Years of experience.** From Lesson 14.2, ten years of feature knowledge can be worth less than three years of business thinking.

❌ **Claimed results.** "I got a 12x ROAS" is unverifiable and usually platform-reported (Lesson 12.5).

❌ **Interview confidence.** The most confident candidate is frequently the one who has never had their assumptions tested.

### What does work: the practical check

Give every serious candidate the same real task.

**The brief:**

> "Here is read-only access to a real account (or an anonymised export). Here are the business's margin figures. Spend two hours and send me:
> 1. The three biggest problems you found
> 2. What each is costing per month, with your workings
> 3. What you would do about each, and how long it would take"

**Pay them for the two hours.** It is a professional courtesy and it improves the quality of responses.

### What you learn from the answers

| Signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| Did they ask for the margin figures before starting? | 🟢 They understand Module 0 |
| Did they quantify findings in money? | 🟢 Business thinking |
| Did they check conversion tracking first? | 🟢 They know what matters (Lesson 8.4) |
| Did they lead with impressions and CTR? | 🔴 Vanity metrics |
| Did they criticise whoever built it? | 🔴 Poor client instinct (Lesson 13.4) |
| Did they say "I would need to test that"? | 🟢 Honesty about uncertainty |
| Did they estimate effort for each fix? | 🟢 Practical |
| Was it a list of 30 undifferentiated findings? | 🔴 No prioritisation |

**One two-hour task tells you more than five interviews.**

### The questions worth asking

**1. "Tell me about a time you were confidently wrong."**
Anyone who cannot answer has either not been tested or is not honest.

**2. "A client's conversions dropped 40% overnight. What do you check first?"**
The right answer is tracking (Lesson 8.4). Anyone who starts with bids has not learned the lesson.

**3. "How would you know if a channel was worth running?"**
Listen for whether they distinguish attribution from incrementality (Lesson 13.2).

**4. "A client asks you to double the budget. What do you do?"**
Listen for whether they check impression share first (Lesson 8.1).

**5. "What would you refuse to do?"**
The best candidates have a line. The concerning ones do not.

## The Training Path

A new hire should not be given accounts on day one.

### Weeks 1–2: Business foundations

**Module 0 only.** No platform work.

They should be able to calculate contribution margin, breakeven ROAS and maximum CAC for three real clients before they touch an account.

⚠️ **Most training programmes start with the interface. This is why most media buyers cannot answer a business question.**

### Weeks 3–4: Observation and audit

Shadow existing work. Run the 100-point audit (Lesson 13.4) on an account, supervised. Present the findings internally.

### Weeks 5–8: Supervised execution

Give them the weekly operational work on two small accounts, with every change reviewed before it goes live.

### Weeks 9–16: Independent execution

Their own accounts, with a weekly review. They keep a change log (Lesson 1.3) and justify every change.

### Months 5–12: Depth

One specialism at a time: measurement, creative, or a channel. Depth in one area beats shallow familiarity with all.

### The competence checkpoints

| Milestone | Test |
|---|---|
| Month 1 | Can calculate unit economics for any business |
| Month 2 | Can complete a full audit unaided |
| Month 3 | Can build a campaign from scratch (Lesson 4.6) |
| Month 6 | Can diagnose a performance decline correctly |
| Month 9 | Can design and run an experiment |
| Month 12 | Can present to a client without supervision |

## Dividing the Work

Three models. Choose on purpose.

### Model A: By account (pod)

Each person owns a set of clients end to end.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Deep client knowledge | No specialisation depth |
| Clear accountability | Inconsistent quality across accounts |
| Simple to manage | Everyone must know everything |
| Good client relationships | Key person risk per account |

**Best for:** small teams, up to about 4 people.

### Model B: By function

One person does all measurement, another all creative, another all execution.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Real depth per function | Nobody owns the client outcome |
| Consistent quality | Coordination overhead |
| Efficient at scale | Weaker client relationships |

**Best for:** larger teams, 8+.

### Model C: Hybrid

Account leads own client relationships and outcomes. Specialists provide measurement, creative and technical work across all accounts.

**Best for:** most agencies between 4 and 15 people. **This is usually the right answer.**

⚠️ **Whichever model, one person must own each client's outcome.** Shared ownership means nobody notices when something breaks.

## The Mistakes That Make Teams Worse

A badly built team performs worse than one good individual. Here is how.

### Mistake 1: No documented standards

Everyone does things their own way. Quality varies by whoever touched the account last.

**Fix:** the standards from Lesson 10.1 — naming, structure, settings, onboarding — written down and enforced.

### Mistake 2: No change log

With one person, memory works. With four, it does not. Nobody knows who changed what or why.

**Fix:** the change log (Lesson 1.3), mandatory, with the reason and expected result.

### Mistake 3: Too many people touching one account

Two people making changes to the same campaign in the same week guarantees you learn nothing, and it disrupts learning phases (Lesson 5.1).

**Fix:** one owner per account. Others advise; they do not edit.

### Mistake 4: Hiring for the tool, not the thinking

A team of people who know every setting and cannot read a P&L (Lesson 14.2).

**Fix:** hire and train on Module 0 first.

### Mistake 5: No review of decisions

People make changes, nobody checks whether they worked, and nobody improves.

**Fix:** monthly review of the change log. Compare expected results to actual. **This single practice improves a team faster than any training.**

### Mistake 6: Growing headcount instead of systems

Adding a person to solve a process problem creates two problems.

**Fix:** before hiring, ask what could be automated (Lesson 11.2) or standardised. Bilal added 7 clients without adding staff (Lesson 10.1).

### Mistake 7: No specialisation as you grow

At 10 people, everyone still doing everything means nobody is really good at anything.

**Fix:** move to the hybrid model and build real depth.

## 📖 Real-World Example: Two Teams at the Same Size

Two agencies reached 6 staff and 20 clients in the same year.

### Agency A: hired to keep up

Every time workload rose, they hired. Six people, all doing broadly the same work, each owning 3–4 clients.

**No documented standards.** Each person had their own naming convention, structure preference and reporting format.

**No change log.** When performance changed, nobody could reconstruct why.

**Hiring rules:** certifications and claimed experience.

**Training:** two days of shadowing, then their own accounts.

**Results at 18 months:**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Clients | 20 |
| Staff | 6 |
| Clients per person | 3.3 |
| Annual client churn | 38% |
| Average client score (100-point audit) | 54 |
| Score variance across clients | 31 to 79 |
| Staff turnover | 2 of 6 in 18 months |

**That variance row is the finding.** Client outcomes depended almost entirely on which person they were assigned to.

**Their best practitioner produced 79-scoring accounts. Their weakest produced 31.** Clients experienced this as luck.

### Agency B: built systems, then hired

They reached 14 clients with 3 people before hiring anyone new — because they had automated monitoring (Lesson 11.2), standardised onboarding (Lesson 10.1), and templated reporting (Lesson 8.2).

**When they did hire, they hired an analyst, not another operator.** Measurement work had become the limit.

**Standards documented:** naming, structure, settings, audit, onboarding, reporting, change log format.

**Hiring:** the paid two-hour audit task. They rejected two candidates with strong CVs who could not quantify a finding in money.

**Training:** the full path above. New hires had no accounts of their own until week 9.

**Structure:** hybrid. Three account leads owning client outcomes, one analyst, one creative, one operator supporting all accounts.

**Results at 18 months:**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Clients | 24 |
| Staff | 6 |
| Clients per person | 4.0 |
| Annual client churn | 11% |
| Average client score | 76 |
| Score variance across clients | 68 to 87 |
| Staff turnover | 0 of 6 |

### The comparison

| | Agency A | Agency B |
|---|---|---|
| Clients per person | 3.3 | **4.0** |
| Churn | 38% | **11%** |
| Average account quality | 54 | **76** |
| Quality variance | **48 points** | **19 points** |
| Staff turnover | 33% | **0%** |

**The variance figure matters most.**

Agency A's clients experienced wildly different quality depending on who they got. Agency B's clients all got roughly the same good service.

**Consistency is what clients actually buy.** A client does not know whether their account scores 76 or 79. They absolutely notice the difference between 79 and 31 — usually by comparing notes with someone else who uses the same agency.

### The staff turnover finding

Agency A lost two people in 18 months. Both said the same thing in their exit conversations: **they felt they were not learning.**

They had been given accounts on day three and left to manage them. They had learned by trial and error, on live client money, with no framework and no review.

Agency B's people had a training path, a specialism, and monthly review of their decisions against predictions (Lesson 13.1).

**Nobody left.**

⚠️ **The training investment that felt slow — eight weeks before independent accounts — was cheaper than replacing two people.** Recruitment, onboarding and the client damage during a handover cost far more than eight weeks of supervised work.

## 📊 Mastery Score

**Level: Master**

Building a team is a different discipline from being good at the work. Many excellent practitioners build poor teams, because they hire people to do what they do rather than building the system that makes people effective.

## 📌 Key Takeaways

- Hire in order: operator, analyst, creative, second operator, strategist. The analyst is usually hired too late.
- Assess candidates with a paid two-hour audit task. It tells you more than five interviews.
- Train Module 0 before the interface. Most media buyers cannot answer a business question because nobody taught them to.
- One owner per account. Shared ownership means nobody notices when something breaks.
- Build systems before headcount. Adding people to a process problem creates two problems.

## 💪 Practical Exercise (35 minutes)

Assess your team's foundations, even if the team is only you.

1. List every standard that is documented versus held in someone's head.
2. Run the 100-point audit score across your accounts. Calculate the average and the variance.
3. If the variance exceeds 20 points, find which accounts are weakest and why.
4. Write the paid audit task you would give a candidate.
5. Find what you would automate or standardise before making your next hire.

## 🎯 Expert Challenge

Design a complete team operating model.

- Build the hiring specification for each role: the capabilities needed, the check task, and the specific signals in a response that show a good or poor candidate.
- Build the training curriculum with the competence checkpoints, and design how each one is tested rather than assumed.
- Design the work division model appropriate to your size now and at double your size, with the trigger that moves you from one model to the next.
- Build the quality assurance system: audit scores per account tracked quarterly, variance monitored, and a defined intervention when an account falls below a threshold.
- Design the decision review process: monthly comparison of predicted outcomes to actual results, per person. This is the single practice that improves a team fastest, and almost nobody does it.
- Then calculate the true cost of a bad hire in your business: recruitment, training, client damage during their tenure, handover cost, and replacement. Most people estimate this at a few months' salary. **The real figure, including client churn, is usually far higher** — and knowing it changes how carefully you hire.
